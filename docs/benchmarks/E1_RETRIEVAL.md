# E-1 — Retrieval at 520 documents, and what it overturned

The F2.2 benchmark used 9 documents. That was enough to locate a problem and far too small to
size it, which is why E-1 was made a precondition for any irreversible retrieval decision. It was
the right call: **the 9-document result was wrong about morphology.**

Corpus: 520 documents (448 in the measured tenant), 170 queries across 9 categories, three
languages, deterministic and regenerated byte-identically on every run
(`benchmarks/e1_corpus.py`, digest asserted by test). Ground truth is produced *by the generator*,
never by a retriever.

## What changed between 9 and 520 documents

| category | F2.2 (9 docs) | E-1 (520 docs) |
|---|---|---|
| morphology | P@1 = 1.00 | nDCG@10 = **0.056** |
| exact / temporal / conflict / crossref | perfect | perfect (held) |

At nine documents almost any lexical match wins by default, because there is nothing to compete
with. At 520 an inflected query term (`retentions` against `retention window`) has to out-rank
hundreds of plausible neighbours, and it cannot. **Small benchmarks flatter retrieval.**

## Measured deltas (this cycle)

| change | P@1 | MRR | nDCG@10 |
|---|---|---|---|
| baseline `5afe8b7` | 0.506 | 0.517 | 0.517 |
| + explicit tie-break (score DESC, id ASC) | 0.506 | 0.520 | 0.524 |
| + document-length normalisation | **0.524** | **0.537** | **0.537** |

No category regressed: exact, temporal, conflict, crossref and adversarial all remain at 1.000.

## The variants that were measured and rejected

Eight standalone scorers were built and run on the same corpus (`benchmarks/e1_retrieval.py`).
The best of them reached nDCG@10 = 0.586 against the engine's 0.517 — but only by trading away the
categories the engine gets right:

| variant | nDCG@10 | why rejected |
|---|---|---|
| BM25 replacing the scorer | 0.474 | temporal 1.000 → 0.877, conflict 1.000 → 0.821, crossref 1.000 → 0.795 |
| BM25 + stemming | 0.542 | same regressions; morphology gain does not pay for them |
| BM25 + char 4-grams | 0.574 | same regressions, plus 10× query latency (0.17 ms vs 0.017 ms) |
| BM25 + stemming + aliases | 0.586 | best overall, worst on the categories that matter most |

The lesson is not "BM25 is bad". It is that the engine's score is more than lexical: the temporal,
exact and authority components are what make conflict and crossref perfect, and a scorer that
replaces the lexical term while discarding those components loses more than it wins. **The gain
available here is in tokenisation, not in ranking** — which is where the next cycle should look.

## Where lexical retrieval still fails, measured

| category | nDCG@10 | reading |
|---|---|---|
| exact, temporal, conflict, crossref, adversarial | 1.000 | solved |
| paraphrase | 0.238 | partial |
| crosslingual | 0.073 | failing |
| morphology | 0.056 | failing |
| synonym | 0.025 | failing |

Morphology is the cheapest of these to fix and needs no model: stemming took it from 0.056 to
0.699 in the standalone matrix. It is not implemented here because doing it inside the engine
means changing the tokenizer, which changes the FTS index format — a migration, not a patch, and
one that deserves its own cycle with its own rebuild-and-verify evidence.

## Known limits of this benchmark

- **No layer-C real-world text.** Vendoring third-party documents would require per-source licence
  provenance that cannot be verified offline. The corpus is synthetic and semi-realistic only, and
  synthetic text is more regular than real prose — these numbers are probably optimistic.
- **Synonym ground truth is strict.** A synonym query expects the one document that states the
  term uniquely, while the corpus contains many documents about the same concept. A retriever that
  returns all of them is arguably right and scores badly here. Treat synonym ≈ 0.025 as a floor.
- **One machine, one run.** No variance estimate, no confidence interval.
